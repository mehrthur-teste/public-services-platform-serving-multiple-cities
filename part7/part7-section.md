# Part 7 – Compare Contemporary Data Processing Paradigms

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relational database systems — typed tables, keys, constraints, joins and transactions. | Citizen requests, departments, maintenance, transport records, multimedia metadata and emergency history. | Consistent record updates, identifier/status lookups, related-record retrieval and transactional workflows. | Large file delivery and broad historical scans competing with live service transactions. | Yes. PostgreSQL remains the main operational store; isolate analytical workloads and keep file contents in S3. |
| Spatial database systems — geometry-aware storage, indexing and spatial operations. | Roads, facilities, service zones, monitoring points and incident locations. | Distance, intersection, containment and spatial joins using appropriate geometries and indexes. | Non-spatial requests gain little from spatial indexes; spatial functionality alone does not manage files or event delivery. | Yes. Use PostGIS within PostgreSQL, with valid geometries, consistent coordinate systems and index-aware queries. |
| Document or semi-structured stores — records represented as nested documents with controlled variation. | Environmental device configuration/events and optional location attributes; transportation configuration uses PostgreSQL JSONB. | Retrieving a complete device document and managing attributes that vary by device type. | Cross-store referential integrity and relationship-heavy retrieval requiring many joins or application checks. | Yes, within a defined scope. MongoDB owns environmental documents; retain operational relationships in PostgreSQL and validate shared IDs. |
| Multimedia management — object/file storage combined with a searchable metadata catalogue. | Images, videos and their links to citizen requests, inspections and traffic captures. | Uploading/retrieving large objects and finding evidence through business-record metadata. | Object storage alone does not perform relational joins or semantic content searches; these require metadata processing or extra services. | Yes. S3 holds files; PostgreSQL holds metadata and evidence links. Coordinate upload states, integrity, access and retention. |
| Time-series processing — observations organised around timestamps and stable series identifiers. | Environmental measurements, traffic readings and temporal transit observations. | Append-heavy ingestion, device/time-range queries and aggregates over comparable metrics and units. | Frequent historical rewrites and complex entity relationships; late or duplicate observations need explicit handling. | Yes. MongoDB time-series collections for environmental readings; indexed PostgreSQL temporal tables for transportation, consistent with Part 2. |
| Rule-based processing — evaluate governed conditions against facts/events and record resulting decisions. | Emergency policies, triggering observations, alerts, response actions and escalations. | Repeatable threshold/policy evaluation, approved rule-version selection and traceable responses. | Bulk archival retrieval or broad analytical aggregation; complex or conflicting conditions can delay event evaluation. | Yes. An external evaluation service uses versioned PostgreSQL rules and records deduplicated alerts; local triggers retain local validation duties. |
| Data warehousing and analytical processing — integrated facts and dimensions organised for reporting. | Historical traffic/environment summaries, service performance and maintenance costs. | Period/city comparisons, grouped indicators and planning queries across consistent reporting dimensions. | Immediate operational updates and current alert decisions; refreshed facts may lag behind source events. | Yes. The final Part 8 recommendation refines the analytical store to Redshift, supported by S3, Glue and EMR. Retain declared fact grains and display the latest successful refresh time. |
| Streaming or event-based processing — continuously process published observations as they arrive. | Incoming traffic and environmental events; inputs to rule evaluation and asynchronous integration. | Decoupled producers/consumers, buffering bursts, prompt event reactions and replay when the chosen event infrastructure supports it. | Broad historical ad hoc queries without retained data/views, or atomic changes across independent consumers. | Yes. Use the Part 2 queue and ingestion/evaluation workers. Choose the broker after workload assessment; manage retries, ordering and duplicate delivery. |

## Comparison and Recommended Roles

We compare eight paradigms because the platform combines accountable transactions, specialised information, historical reporting and continuous observations. They address different responsibilities and can coexist within one architecture. The recommendation retains the Part 2 technologies and distinguishes data storage models from processing approaches. Effectiveness depends on access patterns and consistency needs, rather than using one system for every workload (Kleppmann, 2017).

Relational processing remains central to requests, maintenance and operational histories. Keys and transactions support related updates, such as changing a request status together with its history (PostgreSQL Global Development Group, n.d.-a, n.d.-d). This fits mixed operational workloads, but long regional aggregations can compete with service activity. Spatial processing complements this model: PostGIS supports geographic relationships and index-aware searches (PostGIS Project, n.d.). Nearby-facility and zone-intersection queries benefit, whereas ordinary status lookups still depend on conventional relational indexes.

Document processing suits heterogeneous environmental device configurations and observed events. Retrieving a device and its nested settings can be straightforward, but links to PostgreSQL cities and facilities still need application validation. We therefore keep this scope bounded. Time-series processing addresses a separate concern: repeated observations identified by device and timestamp. Environmental readings use MongoDB time-series collections; transportation retains indexed PostgreSQL temporal tables. This supports append-heavy ingestion and period queries, while update restrictions, late arrivals and duplicate delivery complicate corrections (MongoDB, Inc., n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

Multimedia management separates object contents from structured metadata. S3 supports file storage and retrieval, while PostgreSQL links evidence to business records (Amazon Web Services, n.d.-d). This suits uploads, downloads and metadata searches, but does not itself provide semantic image search. Independent file and catalogue failures require verification and reconciliation. Its responsibility differs from the relational store even though both participate in one evidence workflow.

Rule-based processing applies approved conditions to incoming observations and records the exact rule version behind each alert. This provides traceable decisions, but requires conflict checks and protection against repeated evaluations. Streaming processing transports and processes those observations continuously. Apache Kafka documentation illustrates producers publishing events and consumers processing subscribed streams (Apache Software Foundation, n.d.). Our recommendation adopts the event-processing paradigm through the queue and workers already proposed, while leaving the broker choice open. Buffering can absorb bursts, but ordering, replay, consumer failures and duplicate handling remain design responsibilities (Kleppmann, 2017).

Analytical processing consolidates selected operational changes into shared dimensions and daily facts. This supports planning comparisons across cities and periods, following dimensional modelling principles (Kimball & Ross, 2013). It is less appropriate for immediate case updates or current emergency decisions because refreshes can lag. Streaming supports prompt reactions; the warehouse supports broader historical interpretation. Retained source observations and documented reporting grains connect the two.

We recommend all eight paradigms within these defined roles. Their combination supports operational consistency, specialised retrieval and timely monitoring while preserving an analytical view for planning. Maintenance must coordinate identifiers, units, timestamps, access controls and integration failures across the participating components.
